# 1. Introduction

Foundation models and large language models have rapidly become important components of contemporary artificial intelligence systems. Their ability to generate text, reason over instructions, produce code, summarize information, and support decision-making has encouraged growing adoption across enterprise environments. However, the performance of these systems depends not only on the underlying foundation model but also on the workflow architecture through which the model is deployed.

In many practical applications, foundation models are no longer used only as single-turn text generators. Instead, they are increasingly embedded within agentic workflows that divide tasks into multiple stages, such as planning, execution, review, reflection, or verification. These workflows aim to improve reliability, task coverage, reasoning structure, and output quality by guiding the model through a more organized process. As a result, agentic AI performance may be shaped by both the selected model provider and the architecture of the workflow surrounding the model.

Recent advances in large language models have accelerated interest in agentic AI systems that extend beyond single-prompt interactions. Rather than treating a foundation model as a standalone generator, agentic systems incorporate structured processes intended to improve decision-making, reasoning quality, and task completion effectiveness. These processes may include planning, decomposition, verification, review, tool utilization, and coordination among multiple agents. As organizations increasingly deploy foundation models within operational workflows, understanding how workflow design influences performance has become an important research and engineering challenge.

The growing adoption of agentic approaches reflects recognition that foundation model capability alone does not determine system effectiveness. Identical models may produce substantially different outcomes when embedded within different workflow structures. Consequently, workflow architecture has emerged as a potentially important design dimension that complements ongoing advances in foundation model development.

This distinction is important for enterprise AI adoption. Organizations often compare foundation model providers when selecting AI systems, focusing on model capability, cost, latency, and integration constraints. However, a model deployed through a simple single-agent workflow may perform differently from the same model deployed through a structured multi-stage workflow. Similarly, a workflow that improves performance for one provider may not produce the same benefit for another provider. Therefore, evaluating foundation models without considering workflow architecture may provide an incomplete view of real-world system performance.

Agentic workflow architectures introduce a second layer of design beyond model selection. A Basic Agent workflow relies on direct task execution by a single model instance. A Planner–Executor workflow separates task planning from task execution, allowing the system to generate a structured approach before producing the final response. A Planner–Executor–Reviewer workflow adds a review stage intended to identify weaknesses, omissions, or inconsistencies before finalizing the output. These architectures differ not only in reasoning structure but also in operational requirements, including token consumption, execution duration, and cost.

Previous work in Study 001 evaluated the effect of workflow architecture using a single foundation model provider and showed that workflow design can influence performance across enterprise-oriented tasks. However, a single-provider design cannot determine whether workflow effects generalize across foundation model providers. It also cannot reveal whether workflow effectiveness depends on the underlying model. This creates an important research gap: the relationship between provider selection and workflow architecture remains insufficiently understood.

Despite growing interest in agentic AI, much of the existing empirical literature evaluates workflow architectures using a single foundation model provider. While such studies provide valuable insight into workflow behavior, they offer limited evidence regarding the generalizability of workflow effects across alternative foundation models. This limitation is particularly important because contemporary foundation models differ in training methodologies, instruction-following characteristics, reasoning behavior, context management, and response generation strategies.

As a result, workflow architectures that improve performance under one provider may not necessarily exhibit the same behavior under another. The absence of controlled cross-provider evaluations makes it difficult to determine whether reported workflow benefits represent general characteristics of the architecture itself or interactions with specific model implementations. Addressing this uncertainty is necessary for both researchers seeking robust empirical evidence and practitioners responsible for selecting workflow-provider combinations for production deployment.

The present study addresses this gap through a controlled cross-provider evaluation of agentic workflow architectures. Study 002 compares three foundation model providers—OpenAI GPT-5.5, Google Gemini 2.5 Pro, and Anthropic Claude Sonnet 4.6—across three workflow architectures: Basic Agent, Planner–Executor, and Planner–Executor–Reviewer. The study uses the same benchmark task bank, workflow logic, prompt version, and evaluation procedures across all providers. This controlled design allows the study to examine whether differences in performance are associated with provider selection, workflow architecture, or the interaction between the two.

The study focuses on both effectiveness and operational efficiency. Effectiveness is evaluated using quality score and confidence, while operational performance is assessed using cost, execution duration, and total token consumption. This multi-dimensional evaluation is necessary because higher-quality workflows may also require more model calls, longer execution times, and greater token usage. For enterprise deployment, these operational trade-offs may be as important as quality improvements.

The central research problem addressed in this paper is whether workflow architecture or foundation model provider has a stronger influence on agentic AI performance, and whether workflow benefits remain consistent across providers. This problem is practically important because organizations need evidence-based guidance when deciding whether to improve performance by changing models, changing workflow architecture, or optimizing both together.

This study investigates the following research questions:

RQ1: To what extent does foundation model provider influence answer quality and confidence in agentic AI workflows?

RQ2: To what extent does workflow architecture influence answer quality and confidence across providers?

RQ3: Does workflow effectiveness vary across foundation model providers?

RQ4: What operational trade-offs are associated with increasing workflow complexity in terms of cost, execution duration, and token consumption?

To answer these questions, the study employed a 3 × 3 factorial design involving three providers, three workflow architectures, and 30 benchmark tasks, resulting in 270 official experimental runs. The dataset was validated for completeness, balance across primary experimental factors, and absence of missing values in key outcome variables. Statistical analysis included descriptive statistics, assumption testing, two-way analysis of variance, and effect size estimation.

The results show that workflow architecture exerted a stronger influence on quality score than provider selection alone. However, the significant Provider × Workflow interaction for quality indicates that workflow effectiveness depended on the underlying provider. For confidence, provider selection had a stronger effect than workflow architecture, while the Provider × Workflow interaction was not statistically significant. Operational metrics showed that more complex workflows increased cost, duration, and token consumption, highlighting important efficiency trade-offs.

The main contributions of this paper are as follows. First, the study provides a controlled cross-provider evaluation of agentic workflow architectures using identical tasks, prompts, workflow logic, and evaluation criteria. Second, it quantifies the relative influence of provider selection and workflow architecture on quality and confidence outcomes. Third, it demonstrates that workflow effectiveness can be provider-dependent, supporting the need to evaluate complete workflow-provider combinations rather than models or workflows in isolation. Fourth, it reports operational trade-offs associated with workflow complexity, including cost, latency, and token usage. Finally, it provides a reproducible research package containing frozen datasets, workflow versions, prompt versions, analysis scripts, statistical outputs, tables, and figures.
